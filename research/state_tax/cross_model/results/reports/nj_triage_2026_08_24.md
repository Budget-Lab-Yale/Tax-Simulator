# NJ cross-model triage — first pass

**2026-08-24.** New Jersey was the last state never triaged. This pass does not
close it, but it establishes the direction, which NJ previously lacked entirely:
**the residual is almost certainly ours, it starts at state AGI, and it is
concentrated at the top of the income distribution.**

## Not one cause

No point mass. The largest single difference mode is 14 records out of ~1,960
misses per year. Median signed difference on misses runs +$201 (2017) to +$573
(2020) while the *mean* runs +$9,032 to +$22,128 — a long right tail, not a flat
credit.

## But a clear gradient

Match@$100 by AGI decile, TY2019 clean subset:

| decile | median AGI | match@100 |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 1–2 | $0–4.6k | 0.846–0.896 |
| **3** | **$17.1k** | **0.678** |
| 4–7 | $25.5k–71.6k | 0.907–0.959 |
| 8 | $107.3k | 0.718 |
| 9 | $208.9k | 0.604 |
| **10** | **$852.7k** | **0.403** |

Two distinct problems: a **monotone high-income failure** from decile 8 upward,
which is the dominant one and explains the heavy tail; and a **separate
low-income pocket at decile 3**.

## The stage is state AGI

83% of misses have a state-AGI gap over $100, and we are high on 89% of those.
Only 0.6% of misses have taxable income agreeing, against 78.4% of matches. So
the divergence is established before deductions, exemptions or credits — those
stages agree at roughly the same rate on misses as on matches.

The gap is **two-sided**: our `st_agi` sits above federal AGI (median +$5,119 on
big-gap misses) while TAXSIM's `v32` sits below it (median −$3,546), and the two
medians do not sum to the $16,962 median gap. It is heterogeneous, not one
adjustment.

## Three hypotheses tested and refuted

- **Cross-class loss netting** (the PA pattern, which the encoding note predicts
  for NJ): `st_bus_excess` is **zero on every NJ record**, matching and missing
  alike. Whatever `ob_class_floor` is doing here, it is not producing the gap.
- **TAXSIM not implementing NJ's own base**: it does. Among records with Social
  Security, TAXSIM puts state AGI below federal AGI on 66% (ours 61%); among
  records with UI, 89% (ours 86%). Both models exclude what NJ excludes.
- **A single income component**: correlations between the AGI gap and every
  observable component are **all below 0.06**. The misses are simply richer and
  more complex records — `ei1` median $74.2k against $40.8k, `ei2` present
  1.74× as often, `txbl_int` 2.27×, `n_dep` 1.77×.

## Why it is probably ours

**PolicyEngine shows the same signature independently.** Its window is different
years and a different model, and it agrees: we are high on **84–93%** of misses
across 2021–2024, median +$229 to +$579, match 0.723–0.818. When TAXSIM and
PolicyEngine independently diverge from us in the *same direction* with the same
shape, the parsimonious reading is that the divergence is ours. That is the
inverse of the VT case, where PE agreed with us and TAXSIM was the outlier.

## The hypothesis worth testing next

NJ's distinguishing feature is its enumerated gross-income categories with **no
offset of a loss in one category against income in another and no carryovers**
(`ob_class_floor = 1`). That rule bites hardest exactly where the misses are —
high-income records with business, partnership, S-corp and capital-gain
positions. Being **high** is what over-application of a loss floor produces.

So the next dive should ask whether our floor is applied **more broadly than the
NJ-1040 requires** — for instance flooring within a category where NJ permits
netting, or treating a category as floored that the return does not. That is a
line-by-line comparison of our category construction against the NJ-1040 gross
income lines on decile-10 records, not a search for another point mass.

The decile-3 pocket is a separate thread and probably a different cause.

## Not examined

The pension exclusion was checked and is not the driver: eligible records
(age 62+, pension, AGI ≤ $100k) match at 0.8702 against 0.5233 for the
ineligible, but that difference is the income gradient, not the exclusion —
97.7% of eligibles receive our subtraction.
