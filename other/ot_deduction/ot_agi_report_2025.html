What the columns reveal

  • The bottom of the scale barely participates. Of 60.7M tax units under $20k, only 36.3M file at all, 1.0M claim the deduction, and just 0.25M see any tax cut — most owe no income tax for a deduction to offset.
  • Take-up converts to benefit above ~$40k. From $40k up, claimants and benefiting returns nearly coincide; the gap lives entirely in the $0–40k bands.
  • Average benefit climbs the income scale — from $120 at the bottom to $3,008 in the $200k+ group — tracking higher marginal rates and larger overtime amounts up to the $12,500 / $25k-joint cap.
  • The single largest block of dollars is the $200k+ group ($3.7B), mostly joint filers still partially eligible above the $150k single phase-out threshold.

Source: The Budget Lab at Yale, Tax-Simulator (vintage ot_behavior_2025, full sample, conventional). Calendar year 2025. Benefit = income tax in a no-deduction world minus income tax under current law with the deduction and the induced-overtime behavioral response (ot/france_full, intensive-margin semi-elasticity ≈ −1.47, no phase-in so 2025 carries the full steady-state effect — a modeling choice to match the 2026 view, not a first-year forecast). The response reclassifies pay as overtime rather than adding hours, so wages, AGI, and payroll tax are unaffected. The deduction is scheduled to sunset after 2028. The <0 AGI group shows a few thousand returns and ≈ $0 total benefit (omitted average is a rounding artifact).
