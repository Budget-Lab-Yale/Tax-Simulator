What the columns reveal

  • The bottom of the scale barely participates. Of 58.5M tax units under $20k, only 34.4M file at all, 0.9M claim the deduction, and just 0.17M see any tax cut — most owe no income tax for a deduction to offset.
  • Take-up converts to benefit above ~$40k. From $40k up, claimants and benefiting returns nearly coincide; the gap lives entirely in the $0–40k bands.
  • Average benefit climbs the whole way up — from $98 at the bottom to $2,923 in the $200k+ group — tracking higher marginal rates and larger overtime amounts up to the $12,500 / $25k-joint cap.
  • The single largest block of dollars is the $200k+ group ($4.0B), mostly joint filers still partially eligible above the $150k single phase-out threshold.

Source: The Budget Lab at Yale, Tax-Simulator (vintage ot_behavior_2026, full sample, conventional). Calendar year 2026. Benefit = income tax in a no-deduction world minus income tax under current law with the deduction and the induced-overtime behavioral response (ot/france, intensive-margin semi-elasticity ≈ −1.47, phase-in shortened to one year so 2026 carries the full effect). The response reclassifies pay as overtime rather than adding hours, so wages, AGI, and payroll tax are unaffected. The deduction is scheduled to sunset after 2028. The <0 AGI group shows a few thousand returns and ≈ $0 total benefit (omitted average is a rounding artifact).
