What does turning on s cost the revenue estimate?

Two reforms — capital gains +5pp and a simple top ordinary rate → 39.6% — each scored with the kg_dynamics/turnover behavior module at s = 0 (channel off) and s = 0.5 (half of the net above-baseline during-life tax is financed out of wealth). Switching the saving-financing channel on shaves $8–9 billion off each reform's 10-year estimate — and almost all of it lands in income tax, not estate.

The headline

Including s > 0 lowers the 10-year conventional estimate by −$8.34B for CG +5pp and −$9.02B for top-rate → 39.6 — about 2% of each reform's revenue. The split is lopsided: ~92% is eroded income tax (the financed-out wealth no longer throws off taxable capital income), a small slice is estate tax (a thinner base for the dying top tail), a sliver is payroll (SECA on eroded pass-through), and $0 is wealth tax — neither reform has a net-worth base for the channel to reprice.

How the channel is isolated

The reform's conventional revenue estimate is conv(reform) − baseline. Baseline is shared across s, so the impact of including s > 0 is exactly conv(reform, s=0.5) − conv(reform, s=0) — baseline cancels and nothing but the saving share differs. Everything is on the conventional pass: the static counterfactual is identical across s by construction, so distribution tables are untouched and the interaction surfaces only in receipts.

Two clean tells that this is the channel and nothing else. At s = 0 the estate and payroll deltas are exactly $0 for both reforms — neither touches estate or payroll law, so without the wealth channel those bases cannot move. And the wealth-tax delta is $0 at s = 0.5 too: the channel reprices only bases the scenario actually has, and neither reform carries a net-worth tax.

It is not a one-time hit — it compounds

The financed-out wealth accumulates year over year, so the eroded balance sheet throws off steadily less taxable income. The annual revenue loss therefore grows monotonically across the window and is still accelerating in 2036 — the 10-year sum understates the steady-state cost.

Why the estate slice is bigger for the top-rate reform. The top-rate forcing falls on ordinary income of high earners — households more likely to leave taxable estates — and it grows with nominal income, so by 2036 it has drained $310B vs CG's $265B and the estate impact is accelerating (−0.01→−0.14 $B/yr). CG's drain is front-loaded on realizers and catches relatively more pass-through wealth, which is why its payroll (SECA) slice is the larger one.

Reading the result — caveats

  • Conventional-only, mechanical. This is the saving-financing channel, not a behavioral elasticity. Static estimates and distribution tables are unchanged; the interaction is a conventional-side receipts effect by design.
  • The 10-year figure understates the estate piece. The drain is still compounding in 2036 and the annual estate loss is accelerating, so a longer window would show a larger — and more estate-weighted — total.
  • Wealth-tax delta is structurally $0 here. Add a net-worth base (e.g. a Warren / nickel-dime wealth tax) and the channel would also erode that base; these two reforms simply have none.
  • s = 0.5 is the working assumption (1 − MPC), flat across ages and symmetric, per the v1 design. The sign and ordering are robust; the magnitude scales with s.
  • Full sample required — the wealth-dynamics run guard hard-stops any sub-sample, so both legs are 100% of records.
