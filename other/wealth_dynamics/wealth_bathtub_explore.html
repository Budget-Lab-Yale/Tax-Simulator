The Wealth Bathtub

A new mechanical channel lets a tax paid during life drain wealth that would otherwise have compounded — so the eroded stock feeds three smaller bases: the wealth tax that levies it, the capital income it throws off each year, and the estate it leaves at death. I ran eleven scenarios with the switch off (s=0) and on (s=0.5) to see whether capital gains, the wealth tax, and the estate tax interact the way the design intends. They do — and the test surfaced one real bug, now fixed.

The mechanism

One drain, measured against itself

Today the model fixes wealth (an implicit MPC of 1): a capital tax paid during life never touches the stock, so income and capital gains can't feed the estate. The bathtub relaxes that. A share s = 1 − MPC of the net above-baseline during-life tax is financed out of wealth instead of consumption; that deficit compounds at nominal GDP-per-capita growth and is carried forward per cohort cell until it drains the estate and capital-income base.

Three smaller bases
Wealth-tax base · capital income (yearly) · estate at death

Because the channel is conventional-only, the static pass stays the clean law-only counterfactual and the interaction is read off the contrast. The cleanest read isn't conv−static (that also carries the avoidance/kg behavior) but flipping only the saving share: every number below is conv(s=0.5) − conv(s=0), holding law and behavior fixed.

The design

Eleven scenarios, chosen to probe each interaction

Each reform was run at s=0 (channel dormant) and s=0.5 (half of the marginal tax financed from wealth). The estate-only reform is a deliberate control: an estate tax is paid at death, not during life, so it never enters the forcing — the channel should do nothing.

The point of the feature

One drained dollar, three smaller bases

The reason to build this is that a single wealth drain shows up in more than one place. Here is the Warren tax in 2035: the same eroded stock costs revenue in the wealth tax that caused it, in the income tax on the capital it would have thrown off, and in the estate tax at death. A model with fixed wealth (MPC=1) would score only the first and miss the other two.

Why the drain ordering is on D_alloc, not net worth. For the capital-gains run, the change in total net worth equals the deficit exactly (kg behavior doesn't touch the stock). For the wealth taxes it's a bit smaller — the avoidance module re-optimizes on the post-haircut base, partly offsetting. So the cumulative deficit D_alloc is the clean measure of wealth removed, and it orders correctly: nickel-dime $2.35T > Warren $1.96T > cap gains $0.23T by 2035.
The control

An estate tax alone does nothing — exactly

This is the sharpest test of whether the channel is wired correctly. Restoring 2009 estate parameters roughly doubles estate revenue (≈$71B → $114B by 2035 vs. current law's $36B → $57B). But the estate tax is paid at death, so it never enters the during-life forcing ΔT⁰. With the switch on, the channel should produce zero wealth erosion. It does — to machine precision:

Cross-base amplification

The same drain costs more when the estate tax is bigger

Stacking the Warren wealth tax on the 2009 estate tax isolates the interaction beautifully. The during-life drain is identical to Warren-alone — same D_alloc, same wealth-tax and income-tax loss — because the estate reform doesn't change any during-life tax. Only the estate consequence differs: each eroded dollar of bequest would have been taxed at the higher 2009 rate, so more estate revenue is lost.

The aggressive case

Nickel-and-dime: bigger tax, bigger drain, and a subtle self-damping

The 5%/10% wealth tax drains more wealth than Warren ($2.35T vs $1.96T by 2035) and costs more across every base (wealth −$23.2B, income −$10.1B, estate −$2.56B in 2035). But its net-worth change is a hair smaller than Warren's — the kernel's wealth-tax feedback term damps the deficit's compounding harder when the rate is higher (eroded wealth escapes a steeper levy), and the larger avoidance response offsets more of the stock. Both effects are correct, and neither is large enough to reorder the mechanical drain. No haircut hit the fmax=0.9 clamp for any wealth-tax run (max |f| ≈ 0.045).

One edge worth knowing about (not a bug). The capital-gains run does hit the 0.9 clamp for a handful of records (29–67 of the weighted sample, depending on year): low-net-worth filers with a large one-time realized gain, whose financed deficit briefly exceeds 90% of their thin balance sheet. The guard clamps and logs rather than crashing, and the dollar impact is negligible — this is exactly the sparse lumpy-realization case the code documents.
Found while testing

A real bug: r_total only read projections.csv

The first launch crashed in the wealth pre-pass with "nominal GDP/capita growth missing for year 2025". wealth_dyn_read_rtotal() built the growth path (gdp_t/gdp_{t−1})/(pop_t/pop_{t−1})−1 from projections.csv alone (2026–2099). So any pre-projection lead-in year had no predecessor — and even the first projection year (2026) had none, which the old code papered over with a lead()-backfill hack.

Fixed by splicing historical.csv (1970–2025) ahead of projections — the exact idiom kg_dynamics.R already uses for its CPI/Treasury series — so every year differences off a real prior-year level, and the backfill hack is gone. The fix is material, not cosmetic: r_total(2026) moves from the backfilled 4.00% to the true 2025→2026 value of 4.87%.

  • Code: src/sim/wealth_dynamics.R — wealth_dyn_read_rtotal() now splices both files, dedupes on year, and keeps the historical (actual) row on any overlap.
  • Regression test: 3 new cases in test_wealth_bathtub.R (synthetic CSVs spanning the boundary; asserts the boundary year uses the real prior level, not a backfill). 33/33 pass.
  • Verified on the real macro vintage: a 2024–2030 request now resolves to finite, plausible growth (4.0–4.9%).

Caveats

  • s=0.5 is illustrative, not calibrated — it's the design's placeholder saving share. The signs, orderings, and controls are the test; the magnitudes scale with s.
  • Avoidance elasticities are uncalibrated (public −7, private −17, seeded from the standalone). They sit in both the s=0 and s=0.5 legs, so they cancel in the interaction delta, but they do set the wealth-tax levels.
  • kg calibration is stale vs this Tax-Data vintage (pinned to 2026050315; run on 2026060918) — the pre-pass emitted the expected staleness warning. Again, it's common to both legs of the cap-gains contrast.
  • No FY lead-in: the sim starts in the policy's first year (2026), so first-year FY receipts are slightly undercounted. Immaterial here — the analysis uses calendar-year liability levels and s-deltas. (And the bug fix now permits a 2025 lead-in if you want proper FY-2026 receipts.)
