Death taxes and the lock-in effect

A one-dollar simulator of the choice between realizing a capital gain now and holding it until death. Step-up at death is the engine of lock-in; the estate tax and an annual wealth tax both shrink the prize for waiting. Auten and Joulfaian measured the estate-tax version of this in 1982 probate data — nothing new under the sun.

Take one dollar of unrealized gain with zero basis. Selling today pays the capital-gains tax; holding until death means the gain is forgiven by step-up, but along the way the full dollar sits in the wealth-tax base, and at death it lands in the estate-tax base. The simulator tracks both strategies to the end and asks what the holder's heirs actually receive. The gap between the two outcomes is the lock-in wedge — the tax saving a holder gives up by selling — and everything below is a consequence of how the dials move that wedge.

Why the wedge is what it is. Holding to death keeps the government's unpaid τ share invested in the holder's name. The estate tax takes e of that share once, at death. A wealth tax charges τw on it every year it is held — selling would have removed it from the taxable base — so T years of custody scale the prize by (1 − τw)T. The wedge is τ · (1 − e) · (1 − τw)T per dollar of gain, against a full τ when death taxes leave this margin alone. In the production model these are the death value F = τ(1 − e) and the annual carrying cost h = τw·τ inside the realization model's dynamic program.

What the toy holds fixed. Zero basis; a known death date; wealth tax paid out of the position; present values discounted at the asset's own return, which nets out the ordinary time-value benefit of deferral and isolates the death-tax channels. The regime is step-up throughout — under deemed realization the prize itself goes to zero and this margin disappears.

The evidence. Auten and Joulfaian (2001, Journal of Public Economics) match income and estate tax returns for wealthy 1982 decedents across the ERTA rate changes and estimate a realization elasticity with respect to the estate rate of 0.36 (0.29–0.63 across specifications) — higher estate taxes unlock gains during life. They describe two channels. Bequests become expensive relative to own consumption when e > τ, and the effective transaction cost of rebalancing falls to τ(1 − e) because gains tax paid shrinks the taxable estate. The simulator (and the production model) prices the second channel; the first, a preference margin over consumption versus bequests, sits outside a tax-cost model — one reason the toy's implied elasticity runs below their point estimate.
