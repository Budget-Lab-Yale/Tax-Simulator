How much revenue is there at the top?

There are many ways to raise taxes on the rich, and each comes with its own standalone revenue estimate. But these policies tax overlapping bases and change how people respond to one another's rates, so a package does not raise what its parts raise separately. It can raise less, where bases overlap, or more, where one policy shuts down the escape routes from another.

This page quantifies those interactions: eight top-tax policies with adjustable rates and thresholds, combined however you like, using the Budget Lab's tax microsimulation model. Revenue figures are cumulative over the decade selected below, shown in dollars and as a share of that decade's GDP.

Where it comes from

The package's revenue, base by base

Each bar is one tax base. Segments show which policy raises revenue into that base (right of $0) or drains it (left), colored by policy; the net across all bases equals the combined-package total. The dashed outline behind each bar is the static ask for that base — where the collected fill falls short of it, the gap is revenue lost to behavior (and where the fill overshoots, the base collects more than it was mechanically asked). Raising one tax routinely drains another base — hover any segment for the mechanism. A neutral segment marks revenue that surfaces only when the policies act together.
Spillovers

What each policy does to the other bases

Each selected policy on its own, at your dial settings, with behavioral responses: where its revenue comes from, by tax type. Segments right of the zero line are revenue raised; segments left of it are revenue drained from other bases as behavior responds. A wealth tax erodes the income-tax, capital-gains, and estate bases through saving and avoidance; a higher corporate rate marks down equity values and with them future capital-gains revenue. These spillovers are part of each policy's own standalone score, which is why they don't show up as package-level interaction above.

The frontier: how much is there, at what price?

Each point below is one package drawn from a lattice over the dial space — every policy at several settings, in every combination. Its horizontal position is what the package achieves: either the revenue it collects over the selected decade, as a share of that decade's GDP, or — toggling the axis — how much it raises the top 0.1 percent's realized effective tax rate on accrual income in 2027, the distributional version of the same question. Its vertical position is its behavioral gain or leakage: the gap between its conventional and static revenue scores, in cents per dollar actually collected — positive when behavior helps, negative when it erodes the take. When some other package achieves more with less leakage, a package is dominated; the packages nothing dominates form the frontier line, which traces the cheapest available route to any given amount of revenue — or of top-end tax burden. Points above the zero line collect more than their static score — taxing gains at death removes the incentive to defer realizations, so revenue comes in above the mechanical estimate. Your current selection is marked. (The ETR axis is an impact-year measure, so it does not move with the decade toggle; leakage still does.)

The capital-gains policy in three dimensions

One surface per death regime: the ten-year revenue attributable to the capital-gains rate change — its stacked-last score, on top of the ordinary-rate change and the death regime — at every combination of the two top rates. Behavioral responses included

  • Capital-gains realization: timing and permanent responses, including how taxing gains at death changes the incentive to defer
  • Conversion of ordinary income into capital gains
  • Entity shifting between pass-through and C-corporation form, including its payroll-tax base effect
  • Underreporting and evasion
  • Charitable giving
  • Wealth-tax avoidance, applied consistently to the wealth, income, and estate bases

Mechanical channels included

  • Part of new taxes is financed out of saving, which compounds over time into smaller estates and less capital income
  • Corporate incidence on households: lower dividends, interest, rent, and pass-through income, and marked-down equity values that reduce future realized gains
  • Interactions inside the tax calculation itself: rate stacking, base broadening, and revenue moving across bases (income, payroll, corporate, estate, wealth)

Not included: labor-supply responses, macroeconomic feedback, and changes to tax enforcement.

Model output

Model output, interpolated. Figures come from the top-tax dials batch of the Budget Lab Tax-Simulator: each policy run at a set of anchor settings, every pair run together at reference settings, and the four policies that share the capital-gains-at-death channel also run three at a time. Any dial setting in between is interpolated with a solo + pairwise + triple decomposition, and the interpolation is validated against held-out model runs of full multi-policy packages at off-anchor settings; the badge states the verified bound. The corporate dial is linear between 21% and 28% by construction — the off-model corporate input is itself linear in the rate change. Interpolation is most stretched where an annual wealth tax and the estate tax are both pushed to their extreme settings at once: that combination carries a non-linear interaction the decomposition captures only approximately, so those far-corner packages can run up to roughly 2.6% off the corresponding full model run (vs. under 1% across the rest of the space); every setting shown remains a first-decade estimate within the badge's stated bound.

Revenue figures are 10-year cumulative dollars over the decade selected at the top of the page (first, second, or third decade after enactment), with the share of GDP (10-yr revenue ÷ that decade's cumulative fiscal-year nominal GDP) shown alongside. The distribution card always shows 2027, the impact year, regardless of the selected decade. Policies, effective 2027: top ordinary rate · top capital-gains & dividends rate · corporate rate · annual wealth tax (rate and threshold) · treatment of gains at death (step-up, carryover, or taxed at death) · estate tax (top rate and exemption) · repeal of the QBI deduction · elimination of the Social Security taxable maximum.
