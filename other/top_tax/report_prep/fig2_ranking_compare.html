The cash-versus-accrual rate gap depends on which ranking you use, and the two tell opposite stories about the tail

Effective federal tax rates on top groups under both income measures, computed two ways. On the left, groups are fixed by cash income and only the denominator changes. On the right, each group is re-ranked on the measure being used, so the accrual groups contain different households. Both series are in the v5 model output; the report's Figure 2 currently uses the right-hand panel and the interactive uses the left.

Why they differ

Re-ranking moves the group floor and swaps in households with more unrealized income and less tax

fixedself fixedself fixedself fixedself

The tax column is the mechanism. Under fixed ranking the accrual series has the same numerator as the cash series — it is the same households, so only the denominator grows. Under self-ranking the numerator falls as well, because the accrual-richest households pay less tax than the cash-richest ones: at the top 0.01% the tax drops from $358B to $260B while accrual income rises from $1,512B to $1,714B, and both movements push the rate down. The group floor shows how far the selection moves — the top 1% line sits at $0.91M of cash income but $1.64M of accrual income. Cash-basis rates are identical under both conventions, so the two panels differ only in the accrual series. Source: Tax-Simulator, distribution_etrs.csv, 2027 baseline columns.
