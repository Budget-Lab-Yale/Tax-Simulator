# Input for the "Taxing the Top" report — critique and brainstorm

*Prepared against the director's outline (both variants), the methodology memo, VISION v3, and the 30-year atlas run. All dollar figures below are from the model's own runs; treat them as the current best point estimates, not settled numbers. Where a figure comes from a specific dial (e.g., the 2%-over-$50M wealth run in the decade session, not the 1% MVP dial), it is flagged. (Generated by an independent review agent for the report-prep dossier.)*

## A. What the outline gets right (brief)

- **The spine is sound and honest.** "There's real money → raising rates alone hits limits → the limits are partly a policy choice → explore the combinations" is the correct arc, and it lands on the model's genuine contribution rather than on a headline number.
- **It identifies the actual novel finding.** Figure 4/5 — that eliminating step-up both raises revenue *and* lowers the realization elasticity, so the capital-gains Laffer curve is different under each death regime — is the one thing in this project no one else has quantified. Making it the pivot ("fundamental reforms make raising revenue easier") is the right editorial instinct.
- **It leads with the right thesis exhibit.** Parts-vs-whole (Σ static → Σ conventional → package) is the direct visual answer to "how much is *really* there," and the outline puts it early.
- **The caveat discipline is present.** Conventional-only, revenue-not-welfare, state/local-excluded-and-would-look-less-progressive: all three are flagged. That is the Budget Lab voice.
- **It correctly separates "package interaction" from the standalone scores** — even if (see C2) the vocabulary needs tightening.

## B. Angles the report could take

1. **"Policy chooses its own elasticity."** The contrarian-but-true through-line and the project's real intellectual product: the responsiveness of realizations to the capital-gains rate is not a fact of nature, it is downstream of the step-up choice. *Carry it with Figure 5* — three Laffer curves (step-up / carryover / deemed), the revenue-maximizing rate visibly shifting right as death forgiveness is closed. The theoretical point is old; the *quantified elasticity shift* is the new thing.

2. **The leaky bucket, made numerical.** Okun's bucket, but the leak is measured: the conventional-as-share-of-static ratio for the full stack falls **70% → 66% → 61%** across the three decades, and it also falls as you move up within the top. *One chart:* survival ratio by lever and by top group. Turns an abstraction into a gradient you can point at.

3. **Deferred is not the same as gone.** The accounting-identity angle. Because the model tracks the *stock* of unrealized gains, a gain that goes unrealized when rates rise doesn't disappear — it stays in the stock and resurfaces as a later sale or a bequest. *Exhibit:* the destination ledger's "net-gone" slice shrinking as the window lengthens (deferred gains landing in the estate base later). Reframes a big chunk of apparent "avoidance" as "timing."

4. **Where does the money go — disappears or relocates?** The destination ledger (Object A) as its own exhibit. A dollar leaving the capital-gains base is partly conserved (relocates to the estate or wealth base) and partly gone in-window (evasion + not-yet-landed deferral). *This is the honest, convention-free waterfall* — it never claims to know *which behavior* moved the dollar, only where it ended up.

5. **The 10-year window is the wrong lens for a stock problem.** Time-horizon angle. During-life taxes cannibalize wealth/estate bases *later*: the bathtub compounds and deferred gains land at death, so the survival ratio erodes decade by decade. *Exhibit:* revenue by decade ($10.3T / $15.4T / $21.4T conventional, ~2.6% of GDP each) with the falling survival ratio overlaid. The point: a 10-year score systematically mismeasures — and in different directions for rate hikes vs. base-broadening.

6. **A wealth tax is an income surtax in disguise.** The arithmetic that makes the wealth-tax debate legible: at a 4% taxable yield, a 1% wealth tax is a **25% surtax on that asset's income**, a 2% tax a 50% surtax — "levied on the stock, paid out of the flow, and the stock is twenty-five times the flow." *Exhibit:* a small conversion table (wealth rate → equivalent income surtax at 3/4/5% yields). This is also *why* the avoidance elasticities look enormous — they are the ordinary ETI expressed in stock units.

7. **The doubling illusion.** Discipline the "there's plenty up there" intuition: even doubling the top 1%'s effective rate with the base frozen closes only a fraction of the deficit — and the base does not stay frozen. *Exhibit:* two bars, "if the base held" (static) next to "what you'd actually collect" (conventional), against the deficit path. Do it as a side-by-side rather than a strawman-then-knockdown (see E2).

8. **Two rates: what they owe vs. what you collect.** Welfare-vs-revenue distinction, made concrete. The static-liability ETR (the burden a taxpayer bears, envelope theorem) vs. the conventional ETR (what the Treasury actually books). *Exhibit:* the dumbbell — for the reference stack, the top 0.01%'s 2027 accrual ETR goes baseline **23.7% → "ask" 48.9% (static) → "collected" 37.3% (conventional)**, an **11.6-point avoidance margin**. The gap is the price of leaving avoidance routes open.

9. **Base-broadening beats rate-raising.** The efficiency framing that follows from #1. Closing an avoidance margin collects more at today's rates *and* makes tomorrow's rate increases collect more. *Exhibit:* Figure 3 (rate-only: static > conventional, the bucket leaks) set directly against Figure 4 (deemed realization: static < conventional, behavior *adds* revenue). The most revenue-productive reform is not the highest rate; it's the one that changes the elasticity.

10. **The interaction that isn't there — because the overlap is what matters.** Distinguish the small cross-rate *behavioral* interaction from the large *base overlap*. Summing standalone scores overstates capacity not because rate changes interact behaviorally (they barely do) but because each lever's own score already drains bases the next lever wants to tax. *Exhibit:* the three-bar parts-vs-whole, with the honest label that the middle-to-right gap is "overlap," not "interaction."

11. **The same dollar, taxed five ways.** The opening mechanism, stated plainly: the same unit of wealth is corporate profit upstream, capital income during life, a wealth-tax base in between, and an estate at death. *Exhibit:* a single schematic of one dollar passing through five bases, foreshadowing why the bases can't be summed. Good candidate to *open* the report (see D, Alt 1).

12. **Deficit-share as the yardstick, everywhere.** Make every number legible against the thing the report is actually about. The deficit runs **$1.9T (2027) → $3.1T (2036)**; even the entire eight-switch stack raises ~2.6% of GDP per decade and does not close it. *Exhibit:* revenue bars drawn on the deficit path, repeated as a right-margin reference number rather than a one-time mention. Answers the report's own question honestly: this helps; it is not, by itself, the solution.

13. **The estate tax is where during-life taxes go to die.** The cannibalization channel, as a standalone point. A wealth tax and an estate tax partly erode each other's base; stacking them raises less than the sum. *Exhibit:* the wealth-lever destination ledger showing the estate-base drain (in the 2%-over-$50M decade run, wealth drains estate by **−$87B in decade 1 and still ramping**; income tax −$430B, capital gains −$242B). Note the dial: the MVP card is 1%, so these are illustrative of the mechanism's *direction and shape*, not the headline magnitudes.

14. **Billionaires are modeled, not imputed from survey ghosts.** A credibility angle that preempts the obvious attack. The Forbes tail is spliced in one synthetic record per billionaire rather than asking a few SCF households to stand in for the whole tail — and the self-made-vs-inherited *basis* assumption (zero vs. full) is what drives the gains-at-death results. *Exhibit:* a methods sidebar. Worth a paragraph because the deemed-realization story lives or dies on that basis assumption.

## C. Analytical points the outline overlooks or underplays

1. **The Figure 4 sign flip is asserted, not explained — and it is the subtlest thing in the report.** "Sum of static well below sum of conventional" is genuinely counterintuitive after Figure 3, where static is *above* conventional. The reader needs to be told, explicitly, what "static" holds fixed in *this* model: it is the new law's parameters applied with *behavior frozen at baseline* (i.e., baseline-level deferral). Under a rate hike, letting behavior move *shrinks* the base (conventional < static). Under deemed realization, letting behavior move *pulls realizations forward and adds the death tax* (conventional > static). The response doesn't just weaken — it **flips sign**. Put a static-vs-conventional definition box physically between Figures 3 and 4.

2. **"Interactions are very small" and "you can't just add them up" are both true and about different objects; the outline lets them collide.** There are *three* quantities, not two: Σ standalone static, Σ standalone conventional, and the package conventional. The gap that matters (the "you can't sum" gap) is mostly **base overlap** baked into each standalone conventional score, *not* the cross-rate behavioral interaction term (which really is small in the packages shown). If the report says "interactions are small" without qualification, a hostile reader quotes it against the report's own central claim. Name the three quantities and label the gaps.

3. **σ is a residual — the model matches the ETI target by construction and cannot independently decompose the margins.** σ (income conversion) is set so total top ETI = 0.25 with every other margin already on; the confirmation run lands at 0.2508. This means (a) the model does not *independently validate* the conversion channel, and (b) any decomposition of the ETI into conversion / evasion / entity-shifting is convention-dependent. This is precisely why VISION defers "Object B" (mechanism attribution). The report should present *where the money ends up* (Object A, disciplined) and resist presenting *which behavior moved it* as if the margins were separately identified.

4. **The headline package leans on the least-settled module.** Figure 3's package is corporate + capital-gains + dividends all to 25% — i.e., it foregrounds the corporate channel, whose constants are explicitly **Phase-0c placeholders** (only the 0.375 normal-return share is anchored; κ = 0.40, the U.S.-taxable exposure scale, and the equity risk premium are stated priors). Either lead the package exhibit with a non-corporate combination, or show the corporate result with its sweep band (normal-return share ∈ {0, 0.5}) *on the chart*, not in a footnote.

5. **"Conventional" excludes real macro feedback — and the report should say what that boundary means for its own welfare claim.** No labor supply, no saving response to the return, no investment/growth. Critically, the dissaving/bathtub channel is an *accounting identity for how taxes are paid*, not a behavioral saving response — so the report displays wealth-base erosion but not the efficiency cost of lower investment. The welfare-vs-revenue ETR gap (angle B8) is therefore the deadweight loss of the **avoidance response only** — and even that overstates, because the evasion slice is a transfer the evader keeps, not pure DWL. Label the gap precisely.

6. **The wealth-tax elasticities are ceilings that bundle migration — and the cross-base spillovers are the least-disciplined numbers in the whole model.** The memo is explicit: −7/−17 absorb offshore concealment, valuation gaming, restructuring, migration, and some genuinely reduced accumulation, and the model assigns that whole bundle the concealment/valuation *cross-base footprint*. So the wealth lever's own revenue is a conservative-leaning ceiling, but its drains *into* the income and estate bases are softer than any own-base number. The outline features a wealth dial without this asymmetry.

7. **The deemed-realization level is JCT-anchored, not a free model output.** The 25% valuation/compliance discount is calibrated so the model's 10-year deemed-realization revenue (~$695B) sits near JCT's ~$600B. The novel content of Figure 4/5 is the **elasticity shift**, not the revenue level — the level is tuned to an external benchmark. Disclose it so the finding is claimed at the right altitude.

8. **The "top 1% pays Y% realized / Z% accrual" line mixes a cross-section with a lifetime concept and a single volatile year.** The 23.7% / 37.3% / 48.9% figures are single-year (2027) accrual-denominator snapshots; accrual income swings with equity returns, and the accrual denominator includes gains that — under current law with step-up — will *never* be taxed. The honest claim is narrow: *the realized-income ETR overstates the lifetime tax on economic income, because a large share of top income is deferred and, at death, forgiven.* Don't let a single-year accrual ETR become "the real rate they pay."

9. **Inheritance is modeled as gross of estate tax — an assumption that shapes the distribution table.** The heir allocator holds bequest amounts fixed across scenarios and varies only the liability borne; the identifying assumption is that larger inheritances come from larger estates. This is defensible but load-bearing for "who bears the estate tax," and should be flagged wherever the distribution figure appears.

10. **Internal-consistency risk: the memo may describe modules the shipped vintage doesn't contain.** The methodology memo describes an estate own-rate response (Kopczuk–Slemrod 0.16) and a wealth-carry term in the realization Bellman as built; VISION's gate 2 and the decade session's "scope absences" list still say estate own-rate behavior is *out* of the atlas vintage. Confirm that every module the report *describes* is actually *in* the vintage powering the interactive, or a careful reader finds the seam. This is the single most important thing to reconcile before publication.

11. **"Income received" language undercuts the deferral narrative.** Variant 1 says the top 1% "receive" $XX trillion on an accrual basis. Accrued gains are precisely *not* received — that's the entire point of the deferral story three sections later. Use "accrue," not "receive," for the accrual measure.

12. **First-year and near-term figures are contaminated by retiming and FY conventions.** The 0.25 timing share pulls realizations across years around an announced change (nets to zero only under a permanent, uniform change), and estate revenue books in FY t+1. Single-year ETR/revenue snapshots should note they are not steady-state.

## D. Organizational suggestions

**Alt 1 — "The plumbing report" (lead with the mechanism).** Open on the overlap itself, so every later number is read through it.
- §0 The question and the yardstick (deficit path) — one paragraph.
- §1 *The same dollar, taxed five ways* (angle B11) — the overlap thesis, up front, as a schematic.
- §2 How much is there — realized then accrual, now *motivated* by §1.
- §3 Federal taxes: progressive but narrow (the accrual gap).
- §4 Raising rates — the leaky bucket + the doubling illustration + parts-vs-whole.
- §5 Reshaping the bucket — step-up → elasticity (Figures 4/5).
- §6 Explore it (interactive).
- Caveats live as callout boxes attached to the exhibit each one qualifies.
*Interactive:* the payoff, last, but teased with one screenshot in §0. Best if the reader should leave thinking about the *mechanism*, not a single number.

**Alt 2 — "Two questions" (foreground welfare vs. revenue).** Splits the distinction the outline underplays.
- Part I — *How much can you collect?* concentration → rates → leaky bucket → parts-vs-whole.
- Part II — *What does it cost to collect it?* the two-ETR gap, avoidance DWL, and the efficiency case for base-broadening.
- Part III — *Putting it together:* the interactive and packages.
*Interactive:* Part III. Lead with revenue (concrete) before welfare (harder). Use this if the author wants the efficiency argument to carry equal weight; skip it if the report is primarily a revenue-accounting piece.

**Alt 3 — the director's structure, tightened (minimal-change, recommended).** Keep the five-beat spine and make four moves: (a) pull parts-vs-whole *into* the "raising rates" section as its thesis exhibit rather than treating it as one figure among many; (b) render Figures 3 and 4 as an explicit before/after pair with the static-vs-conventional definition box *between* them (per C1); (c) promote the deficit yardstick from a one-time mention to a recurring right-margin number; (d) in the income section, lead with realized income (solid ground) and treat accrual as "and it is even larger — with considerably more uncertainty."

**On the recurring questions:**
- *Interactive placement:* end of the piece as the payoff, with a single teaser image early. It is the destination, not a section peer.
- *Lead with income or the deficit?* Open with the **tension** — large deficits *and* concentrated income — in the first two sentences, then resolve toward "less than you'd think." Variant 2's "the country is rich" is a good first line; follow it immediately with the deficit so the reader feels the pull before the letdown.
- *Where caveats go:* attach each to the exhibit it qualifies (corporate placeholder → on the corporate chart; conventional-only → on the first revenue chart; wealth ceiling → on the wealth dial). A caveat ghetto at the end of §4 gets skipped and reads as boilerplate.

## E. Traps and honesty risks

1. **"No disagreement that income is concentrated."** True for the *realized* level; overclaimed for the *accrual magnitude* and for the *trend*. Auten–Splinter find much smaller and roughly flat top shares after adjusting for tax-base changes, retained earnings, and transfers; Piketty–Saez–Zucman find large and rising — and the accrual figure the report quotes sits squarely in that fight. Concede: the level is robust, the accrual magnitude and the trend are contested, cite both by name.

2. **The doubling-ETR example.** The outline already flags "unrealistically assuming the base were unchanged" — good — but the rhetorical move (build a static number, then say the real one is lower) invites the "strawman" charge. Present the static and conventional numbers *side by side* so the illustration shows both the ceiling and the erosion in one exhibit.

3. **False precision.** $47T/30yr, 37.3% collected, −$87B estate drain: these come from a model with a residually-calibrated σ, placeholder corporate constants, and ceiling wealth elasticities, and the interactive is a surrogate whose errors run to **±2.6% in decade 1 (worse out-decade) and >0.1pp on ETR cells**. Round hard (two significant figures on any behavioral number), band the corporate and wealth results visibly, and state plainly that the explorer interpolates the 128 underlying runs.

4. **"Interactions are very small."** True for the specific packages shown, and directly quotable against the report's own thesis. Never let "interaction" stand unqualified — say "behavioral cross-rate interactions are small; base overlap is large."

5. **"No one else has quantified this."** Soften to "to our knowledge," and frame it as a capability claim ("because the model makes the realization elasticity endogenous, we can trace how it moves with the death regime"), not a claim that the *idea* is new. The deferral incentive under step-up is textbook; the quantification of the elasticity shift is the contribution.

6. **Corporate incidence stated with unearned confidence.** "Ultimately borne by taxpayers across the distribution, disproportionately those with large asset holdings" is defensible, but the split (0.375 normal, ~40% leaking to foreigners/nonprofits/DB plans off household returns) rests on placeholders. Don't let the distribution figure imply precision on corporate incidence.

7. **Accrual ETR as "the real rate."** Overreach. The defensible statement is that under step-up a large share of top economic income is deferred and forgiven at death, so the realized-income ETR overstates lifetime tax on economic income — not that a single-year accrual ETR is what they "really pay."

8. **"Payroll taxes touch little to no income at the top."** True for the OASDI wage cap, but the report's own model includes NIIT (3.8%) and the additional Medicare tax (0.9%), which *do* reach top capital and labor income — and the report features a taxmax lever. Be precise or a critic notes you waved away instruments your own model runs.

9. **Calling the two-ETR gap "the deadweight loss."** It is the DWL of the *avoidance response only*, and even then the evasion slice is a transfer, not pure DWL — and it excludes the classic excess burden of distorting real saving and labor (out of scope under conventional scoring). Label it exactly.

10. **Vintage/methodology mismatch (see C10).** The gravest honesty risk is describing a module in the methodology that isn't in the run behind the numbers. Reconcile before publishing.

**Candidate pull-quotes** (calm economist voice)

3. "How responsive the wealthy are to the capital-gains rate is not a fixed fact of nature. It is, in part, a consequence of how we choose to tax gains held until death."

7. "Closing an avoidance route does two things at once: it collects more at today's rates, and it makes tomorrow's rate increases collect more as well."
