The flip lives in the "Gains at death" row. Both packages contain the same capital-gains rate increase, and in both it collects $234B against a $547B ask — $313B leaks under step-up. Figure 4 adds taxation at death, which collects $706B against a $291B ask (▲$415B): it lands mostly in ordinary income and capital-gains revenue, while draining the corporate and estate bases (left of $0). The two also reinforce each other — the "acting together" row is ~$0 in Figure 3 but +$166B in Figure 4. Source: Tax-Simulator dials-v2 surrogate; per-policy static (sh) and conventional (ch) revenue by head; the interaction row is the package residual (net minus the sum of solos). Corporate 25% is the linear 21→28 interpolation.

Base-centric or policy-centric? Same data, same three questions — pick by the sentence you want the reader to finish. The base version answers "which base does the money end up in"; this policy version answers "what does each policy do, and where does its money go." For the report's argument — that taxing gains at death changes the policy's own behavior — the policy row that overshoots its ask is the more direct telling.
