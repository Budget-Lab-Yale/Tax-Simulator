One spine for three questions: the revenue cascade

A single figure idiom that carries static-vs-conventional, the effect of each policy, and where the money lands — and that works both as a static report figure and as the interactive's live card.

The three things you're trying to show aren't three charts — they're three cuts of one number: the gap between the back-of-envelope estimate (add up each policy's mechanical score) and the truth (score the package as one reform, with behavior). The report's whole argument lives in that gap: "a meaningful amount, but perhaps less than simple calculations suggest" is the gap is negative; the Figure 4 punchline is the gap flips positive.

The unifying idea

Draw that gap as a cascade: anchor at the naive sum on the left, take one step per policy for its behavioral response, land on the conventional sum, take one more step for the package interaction, end on the truth. The same spine answers all three:
  1. Static vs. conventional — the vertical distance from the first anchor to the "conventional sum" anchor.
  2. Effect of each policy — each step, colored by policy (the same colors as the tool).
  3. Flows across bases — the third cut of the same total, drawn below (Fig. C).
The step direction is the message — and it flips between Figure 3 and Figure 4.

Read the endpoints against the first bar. In Figure 3 both behavioral steps point down: raising capital-gains and corporate rates each collects less than the mechanical score, so the package lands below the naive sum ($685B vs. $1,061B). In Figure 4 the capital-gains step still points down, but taxing gains at death points sharply up — removing the incentive to defer pulls realizations forward and adds the tax at death — so the package lands above its naive sum ($1,106B vs. $838B). Same idiom; the flip in direction is the finding. The tiny neutral "interaction" step is the only piece the current three-bar figure shows that this one keeps. Every policy has a footprint across bases, not just its own. A wealth tax fills the wealth base ($3.9T) but shrinks saving and marks down assets, draining the income ($0.45T), estate ($0.09T), and corporate ($0.04T) bases — the net is what the policy actually scores. How it drops into the interactive

The cascade replaces the three totem-pole bars in the current "Parts vs. whole" card. Right now a reader has to subtract bar 1 from bar 2 in their head to see the behavioral wedge, and bar 2 from bar 3 to see the interaction; the cascade draws both wedges as steps. Three things carry straight over:

  1. Hover a step → the mechanism copy you already wrote for the spillover catalog, plus the relocated-vs-gone split (how much of a leak conserves into another base vs. is gone in-window).
  2. The "Where it comes from" base card becomes the cascade's drill-down (Fig. C), so the two cards stop competing as separate idioms.
  3. The decade toggle just re-scales the same spine; the sign-flip story holds across all three decades.
