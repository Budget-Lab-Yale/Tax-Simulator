# [Front matter]

The United States faces an unsustainable fiscal trajectory. Primary deficits are high by historical non-recessionary peacetime standards and growing due to population aging, and interest rates have, in the last few years, risen from the unusually low levels seen in the 2010s. Under these fiscal and macroeconomic conditions, debt is set to grow as a share of the economy without bound.

One common response to this situation is to propose raising taxes on those at the top of the income distribution. This reaction, in part, results from the fact that a meaningful fraction of total income in the economy accrues to the highest-income households. The projected FY 2027 deficit is $1.9T; this number represents the total income of both the bottom 42 percent and the top 0.05 percent.

In this report, we look at the question of how much revenue could realistically be raised from higher taxes on the top, drawing on new features from the Budget Lab's tax model. We begin by contextualizing top incomes within the broader economic and fiscal picture, showing why the definition of income matters for thinking about both inequality and tax policy.

We then show how revenue potential at the top depends as much on tax design as it does on tax rates. Simply raising tax rates would indeed generate meaningful revenue, but revenue potential is limited by several factors: a finite amount of income at the top, the tax code's narrow view of income, and structural issues that allow for leakages through avoidance. Some of these limitations, however, are policy-choice side effects rather than something fundamental about the economy. Most notably, we show that reforming the tax treatment of capital gains at death would unlock substantially more revenue potential from top-tax increases.

The report ends by highlighting a new interactive tool that allows users to construct and score their own combinations of reforms.

## Why look at the top for revenue? Projected deficits are large, and the income distribution is unequal

Those at the top of the income distribution earn trillions of dollars from work and investments annually. Though there are robust arguments rooted in serious methodological disagreements about the exact degree of income concentration and its trajectory in recent decades, there is no disagreement as to whether income is concentrated at the top.

A simple way to measure concentration is to look at "top income shares"— the fraction of all income earned by the top X percent, for example the top 1 percent. But before considering specific statistics, it's important to be precise about what we mean by "income".

One approach for defining income is on a "cash" or "realization" basis, where income includes current-year cash inflows from work and investment (e.g., wages, self-employment earnings, dividends, interest, etc.) as well as realized capital gains. This view of income is based on the timing of when economic value is turned into cash.

Another approach is to define income on an "accrual" basis, which swaps realized capital gains for accrued gains—that is, this year's increase in the value of assets, regardless of when or whether these gains are realized. This view of income is based on the timing of when economic value accrues, not when it is turned into cash. For those at the top of the income distribution, accruals play an outsized role: asset holdings are large relative to traditional wage income, so a greater share of economic income takes the form of asset appreciation rather than cash receipts. And because tax motivations (described below) mean that many of these gains are never realized, a cash-basis measure will systematically understate the top's share of economic income. Therefore, when measuring the amount of income at the top of the distribution, it's important to consider accrual-based measures in addition to more traditional measures based on cash income.

Using a cash income concept, we project that, in 2027, the top 1 percent of tax units will receive $5.2 trillion, or 23 percent of all income—an amount roughly equal to the income received by the bottom two thirds of the distribution. If we instead look at these numbers on an accrual basis, assuming that in 2027 assets appreciate near historical averages, our estimate rises to $8.7 trillion, or 27 percent of all income. From this income received, we estimate that those taxpayers will remit $1.4 trillion to the federal government through income, payroll, corporate, and estate taxes. Figure 1 shows our estimates of income for different, overlapping groups at the top of the income distribution, under both cash and accrual measures.1

To put these numbers in context, the annual deficit in 2027 is projected to be $1.9 trillion, rising to $3.1 trillion in 2036 as the population ages and the cost of servicing accumulated debt rises further.

## Federal taxes are progressive, but are narrow in important ways

Under current law, those at the top of the income distribution are subject to various federal taxes:

- individual income taxes, a progressive schedule of rates on a measure of cash income, though income from investments is generally taxed more lightly than income from work;
- payroll taxes, which tax earnings from work regressively;
- corporate taxes, which are ultimately borne by some mix of investors and workers, but disproportionately tax those with large asset holdings and so are progressive on net;
- estate taxes, a very narrow but steeply progressive tax on a small fraction of the richest Americans at death (though trust-related avoidance strategies limit its reach in practice); and
- other taxes not modeled in this report, like excise taxes and tariffs, which are generally regressive.

Together, these bases form a net-progressive federal tax system—that is, a system where taxes as a share of cash income are higher for high-income households than low-income households. We estimate that the top 1 percent pays about 26 percent of their cash income in these taxes, generating almost a third of all revenue from these sources.

But even still, the federal tax system misses a large fraction of income at the top of the distribution entirely. This is for two inter-related reasons: the realization principle, and the step-up in basis at death.

The realization principle simply refers to the fact that gains are taxed on a realization or cash basis, not an accrual basis. This means that investors with unrealized gains have some degree of discretion over when they pay tax. If it were guaranteed that an investor would eventually pay tax on those gains, this discretion would mostly be a matter of timing.

But the tax treatment of unrealized gains at death creates an escape valve for taxes on accrued gains. At death, the cost basis on an asset transferred to an heir is, from the tax code's perspective, "stepped up" to its fair market value. This means that, for those wanting to transfer wealth to their children, there is a strong incentive to never sell appreciated assets. This provision generates a large hole in the income tax: trillions of dollars of economic income are never subject to tax. While all income groups benefit from this tax treatment in principle, accrued gains represent an outsized component of income at the top (especially when excluding primary residences). The step-up in basis therefore reduces the progressivity of the tax system and results in lower revenues for the federal government.

This dynamic is a central reason why these federal taxes are less progressive and generate lower effective tax rates when measured on an accrual income basis, not a cash income basis. Figure 2 shows effective rates under both measures of income. The top 1 percent's rate falls from 26 to 15 percent when moving from cash to accrual income.

## Revenue potential from raising existing taxes at the top is meaningful but limited

How much additional revenue could be raised by a package that raises existing tax rates on the top, but doesn't make any more fundamental reforms to the tax system? The short answer is: a meaningful amount, but perhaps less than what simple calculations would suggest. Revenue potential from this kind of package—for example, raising the top ordinary income rate, the top capital gains rates, and the corporate rate together—is limited for three main reasons.

The first and most basic reason is that there is a finite pool of pre-tax cash income, as shown above. To illustrate this, consider a package which doubles the effective tax rate on the top 1 percent by raising it from 26 to 52 percent—an increase that would, were it to hold lower income groups harmless, would require marginal tax rates in the 66-75 percent range. Under the extreme assumption that the tax base was unchanged despite such a large increase, the additional revenue (about 4 percent of GDP) would total still only close about two thirds of the deficit over the next ten years (about 6 percent of GDP).

The second reason is that taxpayer behavior would change, reducing the tax bases and thus the associated revenue potential. Under the current tax structure, there are ample opportunities to avoid or evade the full burden of a tax-rate increase, resulting in leakages where a dollar of newly assessed tax leads to less than a dollar in collections. Examples of this behavior include: when capital gains rates rise, some taxpayers respond by delaying or simply never realizing capital gains; when the corporate tax rate rises, some businesses recharacterize as pass-throughs; when ordinary tax rates rise, some labor compensation is restructured to look like capital gains; when pass-through taxes rise, more income simply goes unreported. These behavioral responses are well-documented under the current tax system.

The third reason is that tax bases interact mechanically. When multiple taxes are assessed on the same flow of income or stock of wealth, an increase in one tax results in less revenue from the other tax. For example, when the corporate income tax rate goes up, after-tax profits fall, leaving less income for shareholders and potentially reducing equity prices, which in turn means less in income taxes and estate taxes—about 8 cents of loss per new dollar of corporate tax, per our estimates. A related dynamic is the mechanical decumulating effect of new taxes on wealth. A sharp tax increase on the top would be financed in part by lower saving (as opposed to lower consumption), which would lead to less top wealth in future years, in turn reducing taxable investment income flows and taxable estate values. This dynamic compounds over time, small at first but meaningful at multi-decade horizons.

(A fourth reason, though not reflected in our conventional budget scores shown in this report, is that taxpayers might respond by reducing substantive economic activity, like reducing labor supply, saving rates, or entrepreneurship. These margins, which can create macroeconomic feedback effects, are the domain of dynamic scoring.)

To walk through an example of these dynamics in practice, Figure 3 shows the estimated revenue impact of raising the top capital gains rate (excluding NIIT) from 20 to 25 percent first and then raising the corporate tax rate from 21 to 25 percent. The slim, translucent bar represents the static revenue impact—i.e., the first-order tax increase before considering the leakages described above—and the wider bar shows actual revenue raised. Each revenue bar is broken down into which tax base it raises revenue from.

The capital gains increase loses 0.08pp of GDP to behavioral feedback, mostly due to lower realizations. This policy also causes some business owners to recharacterize C-corporate income as pass-through income, leading to a slight drop in corporate tax revenues and a tiny increase in ordinary income.

The corporate tax rate increase reduces after-tax profits and thus reduces stock values and corporate distributions (dividends and buybacks). This interaction between tax bases causes offsetting declines in capital gains taxes, ordinary income taxes, and a very small drop in estate taxes. As with capital gains above, this policy further incentivizes business income movement into pass-through income form, draining corporate tax receipts and boosting ordinary income taxes.

The net effect of these dynamics is that the actual score for the package ends up being less than two thirds of the naive static estimate.

## But certain fundamental reforms make raising revenue from the top easier and more efficient

Some amount of tax avoidance and noncompliance is inevitable. But some of the limitations described in the previous section are, in an important sense, a policy choice.

Fundamentally, tax avoidance strategies are made possible when similar economic activities are taxed at different rates, encouraging taxpayers to change their behavior or superficially recharacterize their income to qualify for the lower rate. It's well understood that reducing or eliminating these arbitrage opportunities would cut down on economically wasteful avoidance under the current set of rates, raising revenue mechanically. But less appreciated is a second effect: closing off avoidance channels makes the tax base less responsive to rates. In other words, certain reforms don't just collect more at current rates, they lower the behavioral offset from further rate increases—raising the maximum revenue the tax can collect or allowing any given revenue target to be hit with smaller rate increases.

The most consequential example of this dynamic involves the tax treatment of unrealized gains at death.2 Evidence shows that under current law, investors simply choose to sell fewer assets during life when capital gains tax rates go up. This high degree of responsiveness to tax—what economists call the "realization elasticity"—means that the highest you can raise rates before starting to lose revenue is limited: estimates rooted in the empirical realization literature put it somewhere in the low-to-mid 30s, and our model, which lets several avoidance margins operate at once, finds revenue peaking near a 40 percent rate and declining beyond it. Either way the revenue-maximizing rate sits at or below the current top rate on ordinary income. So, step-up in basis limits the capital gains tax usefulness as a revenue tool, and because capital gains make up an outsized share of income at the top, it therefore limits the scope for raising taxes on the top more broadly.

But, as described above, having a high realization elasticity is a policy choice. In the absence of the step-up in basis, the incentive to defer realization would be greatly diminished. Investors still might avoid taxes by strategically retiming realizations when income is low or deferring tax payments to take advantage of compounding returns, but no longer would deferral enable total erasure of tax. One version of this policy change would be to institute carryover basis, where heirs would retain the original cost basis of the decedent and pay tax on gains when sold; another, more aggressive change would treat death as a deemed realization event.

Figure 4 shows the same capital-gains rate and corporate rate package—except now scored in the context of a tax system where step-up in basis is replaced with a policy where unrealized gains are taxed at death. In the prior experiment, where the policy context was the current-law step-up regime, raising the top capital gains rate by 5 percentage points yields 0.06 percent of GDP in the third decade (FY 2047-56), about $500 billion. In a world with deemed realization at death, we estimate this same policy change would raise 0.11 percent of GDP, about $930 billion—nearly twice as much, and it collects a larger share of its static estimate. The package as a whole collects about 95 cents of every statically-assessed dollar in this regime, against 63 cents under step-up.

Another way to visualize this dynamic is with the capital gains Laffer curve, which plots revenue raised at different tax rates. Figure 5 traces third-decade revenue as the top capital gains rate rises from 20 to 50 percent, under each of the three treatments of gains at death. Under current-law step-up, the curve peaks near a 40 percent rate at about 0.13 percent of GDP and then falls—past that point, each additional point of rate loses money, because it pushes more gains to be held until death, where they escape tax entirely. Replacing step-up transforms the curve twice over. First, it raises the starting point: taxing gains at death collects about 0.17 percent of GDP at today's 20 percent rate, before any rate increase at all (carryover collects about 0.08 percent). Second, it keeps the rate productive: under deemed realization the curve is still climbing at a 50 percent rate, reaching roughly 0.59 percent of GDP—more than four times what the step-up curve manages at its own peak. Carryover basis sits between the two, peaking near 45 percent. The lesson of the figure is that the revenue-maximizing capital gains rate is not a fact of nature; it is a function of what the tax code does at death.

## A new interactive tool makes it possible to explore combinations of reforms

There are many tax instruments that can be used to raise taxes on those at the top of the income distribution, only some of which are discussed in this article. Because it is sometimes important to consider them in a unified way, and because there are many possible combinations of tax policy changes, The Budget Lab has published an interactive tool that allows users to explore them. The interactive focuses on understanding income (either realized or accrued) across the distribution and the tax revenue collected in various policy scenarios. Several policies can be adjusted by the user: the top rates on ordinary income tax as well as capital gains and dividend tax, the corporate rate, the estate tax rate and exemption, the Social Security taxable maximum threshold, the Qualified Business Income deduction, and a wealth tax rate and minimum threshold. In addition, the treatment of unrealized gains at death can be varied between step-up in basis, carryover basis, and taxation at death, with an optional per-decedent exclusion that lets the first $1 million or $5 million of a decedent's gains keep step-up.

At the top of the tool, buttons allow the user to toggle between different ten-year scoring windows, defaulting to the first 10-year window. The left sidebar lets users choose specific combinations of policy counterfactuals, dialing rates and exemptions to desired levels. At the top of the tool, key results (like the 10-year revenue gain) are shown and explained. Note that the tool shows package-level interaction here, not including the interactions that are captured within individual scores, as when a stand-alone corporate tax increase lowers capital gains and dividend revenue. That type of spillover is described by the interactive in a section titled "Spillovers".

The effective tax rate, accessible in the section "Distribution" by clicking the button "Effective rate", shows the effective tax rate with either a cash income (realized) denominator or an accrual income denominator, which can be toggled by the user. At the bottom of the interactive, a distribution card places each package in context: it shows who bears the new taxes across the income distribution, in dollars or as a change in effective rates, on either income basis. A note on method: taxes assessed on decedents—the estate tax and, where selected, tax on gains deemed realized at death—are attributed to heirs in proportion to the tax owed by estates of the size their inheritance implies, so the burden of these taxes appears where the inheritances land.
