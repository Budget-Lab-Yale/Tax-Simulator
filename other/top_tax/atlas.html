How much revenue is there at the top?

There are many ways to raise taxes on the rich, and each comes with its own standalone revenue estimate. But these policies tax overlapping bases and change how people respond to one another's rates, so a package does not raise what its parts raise separately. It can raise less, where bases overlap, or more, where one policy shuts down the escape routes from another.

This page quantifies those interactions: seven top-tax policies, scored alone and in every combination, using the Budget Lab's tax microsimulation model. Revenue figures are cumulative over 2027–2036, shown in dollars and as a share of GDP.

Spillovers

What each policy does to the other bases

Each selected policy on its own, with behavioral responses: where its revenue comes from, by tax type. Segments right of the zero line are revenue raised; segments left of it are revenue drained from other bases as behavior responds. A wealth tax erodes the income-tax, capital-gains, and estate bases through saving and avoidance; a higher corporate rate marks down equity values and with them future capital-gains revenue. These spillovers are part of each policy's own standalone score, which is why they don't show up as package-level interaction above.

The frontier: how much is there, at what price?

Each point below is one of the 127 packages. Its horizontal position is what it collects over ten years. Its vertical position is its leakage price: the gap between its static and conventional scores, in cents per dollar actually collected. When some other package raises more money at a lower leakage price, a package is dominated; the packages nothing dominates form the frontier line, which traces the cheapest available route to any given amount of revenue. Points below the zero line collect more than their static score — taxing gains at death removes the incentive to defer realizations, so revenue comes in above the mechanical estimate.

Behavioral responses included

  • Capital-gains realization: timing and permanent responses, including how taxing gains at death changes the incentive to defer
  • Conversion of ordinary income into capital gains
  • Entity shifting between pass-through and C-corporation form, including its payroll-tax base effect
  • Underreporting and evasion
  • Charitable giving
  • Wealth-tax avoidance, applied consistently to the wealth, income, and estate bases

Mechanical channels included

  • Part of new taxes is financed out of saving, which compounds over time into smaller estates and less capital income
  • Corporate incidence on households: lower dividends, interest, rent, and pass-through income, and marked-down equity values that reduce future realized gains
  • Interactions inside the tax calculation itself: rate stacking, base broadening, and revenue moving across bases (income, payroll, corporate, estate, wealth)

Not included: labor-supply responses, macroeconomic feedback, and changes to tax enforcement.

Model output. Figures come from the 128-combo factorial run of the Budget Lab Tax-Simulator: seven binary switches, effective 2027, ten-year window 2027–2036. Corporate figures use a placeholder off-model input pending the official estimate.

Revenue figures are 10-year cumulative dollars, with the share of GDP (10-yr revenue ÷ 10-yr cumulative fiscal-year nominal GDP) shown alongside. Policies: top ordinary rate +5pp · capital gains taxed at 39.6% · corporate rate 28% · wealth tax of 1% above $50M · deemed realization at death · estate tax at $5M exemption / 45% rate · repeal of the QBI deduction.
