How much revenue is there at the top?

There are many tax policy options for raising revenue from the rich, each with its own standalone revenue estimate. But these policies tax overlapping bases, and they interact — mechanically and through behavioral responses — so a combined package does not raise the sum of the standalone estimates. It can raise less, where bases overlap, or more, where one policy closes another's escape route.

This page quantifies those interactions: seven top-tax policies, scored alone and in every combination, using the Budget Lab's tax microsimulation model. Revenue figures are cumulative over 2027–2036, shown in dollars and as a share of GDP.

Standalone scores vs. the combined package

The sum of each selected policy's standalone score, compared with the score of the combined package run as one reform. The difference reflects base overlap and interaction. Each bar is split by policy: the first two split trivially into the standalone scores; the package bar is split by each policy's Shapley contribution — its marginal revenue averaged over every order in which the package could be assembled, computed exactly from the runs of all sub-combinations. Contributions sum to the package total, so interaction effects — losses and gains alike — are shared among the policies responsible. All estimates are measured against the same current-law baseline.
Order matters

Stacked first vs. stacked last

Each selected policy's revenue at the two extremes of stacking order: first — alone, against current law (its standalone score) — and last — added on top of everything else in the package (the package score minus the package without it). These are the two ends of the orderings the Shapley split above averages over; the gap is the policy's interaction with the rest of the package. A positive gap means the package strengthens the policy — for example, taxing gains at death closes the deferral escape that otherwise drains a capital-gains rate increase.
Spillovers

What each policy does to the other bases

Each selected policy on its own, with behavioral responses: where its revenue comes from, by tax type. Segments to the right of the zero line are revenue raised; segments to the left are revenue drained from other bases as behavior responds — saving and avoidance under a wealth tax erode the income-tax, capital-gains, and estate bases; a higher corporate rate marks down equity values and, with them, future capital-gains revenue. These first-order spillovers are part of each policy's own standalone score (the net figure at right), which is why they don't appear as package-level interaction above.

The frontier: how much is there, at what price?

Every one of the 127 packages, placed by what it collects (horizontal) and its leakage price — the gap between its static and conventional scores per dollar actually collected (vertical). A package is dominated when another raises more revenue at a weakly lower leakage price; the undominated packages trace the frontier, which reads as a supply curve of top-end revenue. Below the zero line a package collects more than its static ask: taxing gains at death removes the incentive to defer realizations, and that efficiency gain anchors the frontier's first several trillion. Leakage price is a heuristic efficiency measure — offsets include cross-base shifts and timing, not only deadweight loss — and corporate figures ride a placeholder input.

Model output. Figures come from the 128-combo factorial run of the Budget Lab Tax-Simulator (7 binary switches; effective 2027; 10-yr window 2027–2036; conventional estimates include capital-gains realization dynamics, ordinary↔gains conversion, entity shifting, evasion, charitable response, wealth-tax avoidance, the saving-financing wealth channel, and on-model corporate incidence). Corporate figures use a placeholder off-model input pending the official estimate.

Revenue figures are 10-year cumulative dollars, with the share of GDP (10-yr revenue ÷ 10-yr cumulative fiscal-year nominal GDP) shown alongside. Policies: top ordinary rate +5pp · capital gains taxed at 39.6% · corporate rate 28% · wealth tax of 1% above $50M · deemed realization at death · estate tax at $5M exemption / 45% rate · repeal of the QBI deduction.

On "capital gains & dividends." It is not a separate receipt — the model books revenue into seven destinations (income tax, payroll, corporate, estate, wealth, VAT, other). It is the preferential-rate slice of the individual income tax (liab_pref + §1250 + collectibles, from totals/1040.csv, both legs), carved out for legibility in the destination ledger only (the effective-rate chart keeps income tax lumped, since the distribution pipeline is static-sourced and doesn't decompose it per percentile); ordinary income tax is the residual, so credits park in the ordinary bucket. Qualified dividends ride with long-term gains; gains taxed at death under the deemed regime join this head.
